# Selwey-Harvey

*A framework for optimal action among actors*

*Original writing — September 16, 2026*

*Working draft*

---

## One — The problem you already know

You, acting within a system, have at some point done everything right and still encountered a ceiling. Applied your capabilities effectively. Made sound decisions given what you could see. Pursued your goals with the tools and information available to you. And still arrived at a result that was less than what you would have desired your effort, intelligence, and context to produce.

This is familiar enough that most actors treat it as a point of fact — an inevitable friction, the cost of operating in a world that contains other actors and forces beyond your control. And so the usual responses follow: increase effort, refine strategy, strengthen your position, secure what you have. These responses are sensible. They often help. And they reliably encounter the same ceiling again, a little further along.

That pattern — capable action producing diminishing returns against a structural constraint — is worth paying attention to. In any particular instance, there are usually identifiable factors at play: strategy, resources, timing, rules, circumstances. Most of them respond to adjustment, and most of the time that's where the useful work starts. Concealed underneath the noise of those factors, there is a structural pattern that is always present and always affecting outcomes, but tends to become visible only when the easier explanations have been exhausted — when the adjustments have been made, the resources applied, the strategy refined, and the ceiling persists. It is worth asking what's generating that residual constraint, and whether something about the frame of optimisation itself might be itself adjusted.

How the frame could be adjusted to increase outcomes is what the rest of this document explores.

---

## Two — Why the ceiling exists

Every actor — every entity that acts, responds, and pursues outcomes — does so from within a bounded perspective. A position in the world, a set of interests, a particular view. These boundaries are real, and they are structural. They define what you can see, what matters to you, and what you're inclined to do about it.

The difficulty is that the system you operate within is larger than your frame, collectively and individually. Other actors are in it. They have their own boundaries, interests, and perspectives. When you act, the effects of your action propagate through the system and encounter those other actors, whose responses in turn change the conditions you're operating in. This happens constantly, at every scale, and much of it is invisible from any single perspective.

Optimising from within your own frame, then, means optimising within a complex, adaptive system your actions affect. Your reasoning may be sound, and still be working from inputs that are smaller than the reality they need to account for. You're solving a local equation in a connected system, and the parts of the system you can't see are generating effects that show up in your results as friction, resistance, opportunities, or ceilings that seem to come from nowhere.

This is true of every bounded actor, not just individuals. Organisations, communities, nations, any collective — all operate from within their own frame, and all encounter the same pattern: diminishing returns that arise from the gap between local optimisation and systemic reality.

The gap is the ceiling. And expedient hyper-optimisation within the existing frame can actually reinforce it — a series of devil's bargains where each local win deepens commitment to the frame producing the constraint. You can lose the war by winning every battle. Expanding what the frame includes is what changes the terms.

## Three — What better looks like

Expanding the frame does not mean abandoning your own interests. This matters, because the most common reason actors resist broader frameworks is a reasonable fear that "think bigger" is code for "sacrifice yourself." A framework that asks any actor to zero out its own interests in service of a larger whole is structurally unstable, because it removes a real node from the system and weakens the diversity the system depends on.

The key to unlock is the holding of three levels of interest in simultaneous consideration. Three levels, specifically: your own, those of the collectives you participate in, and those of the whole — the totality of all sentient actors. Each level has legitimate interests. Each level matters. And none of them gets to completely override the others.

This is closer to a constitutional structure than a democratic one. A democratic arrangement among the three just means two levels can vote against the third, producing a sophisticated justification for zeroing someone out. A constitutional structure establishes inviolable constraints: the tension between these levels is permanent, it is balanced, it is productive, and no resolution is acceptable that eliminates any of them.

The balanced tension between these three levels is where the value is generated, and it is generated through exchange. When you consider your collectives, you gain a more accurate model of the system you're operating in — and your collectives gain the benefit of your distinct perspective and capabilities. When a collective protects the interests of its individual members, it retains the diverse capacities that make it adaptive — and those members gain access to resources and resilience they could never produce alone. When the whole maintains conditions that allow collectives and individuals to function, it preserves the richness that gives it strength — and every actor within it operates in an environment more capable of supporting their goals. Each level's consideration of the others is a contribution that generates returns at every level simultaneously. The value compounds precisely because it flows in all directions.

The framework rests on five tenets:

First, the foundational claim: **agency compels inclusion**. Any actor that acts, responds, and adapts within a system has standing in global accounting. This is the precondition for everything that follows.

Second, **boundaries are real and permeable**. Actors and collectives have genuine, distinct interests. These boundaries are functional and should be maintained. Treating them as impermeable walls extracts a compounding cost on systemic outcomes. High-fidelity channels for insight and value flow across boundaries — ambassadorship — are a necessary structural investment.

Third, **the constitutional tripartite structure**. The individual, the collectives, and the totality of all sentient actors are held in permanent productive tension. No level may be zeroed out or over-minimised by the others. This is constitutional, meaning it operates as an inviolable constraint, rather than a preference to be overridden when convenient.

Fourth, **temporal counterweighting**. Systematic correction against the bias toward short-term action is required. The pressures that drive temporal discounting — mortality, scarcity, urgency — are real, and they consistently distort decisions away from optimal long-term outcomes. The correction does not eliminate the immediate; it prevents the immediate from eliminating the long-term.

Fifth, **self-constrained optimisation achieves deeply optimised results**. Unchecked optimisation logic progressively sacrifices diversity for arithmetic efficiency until the system becomes brittle. The constitutional structure and temporal correction prevent the optimisation engine from consuming the components it depends on — and in doing so, give it access to the unlock that unconstrained optimisation forecloses.

## Four — Testing the model

A framework earns trust by making predictions you can check. Selwey-Harvey makes specific, testable predictions about what happens in systems under different conditions. Rather than argue from particular historical cases, which invite debate about the cases rather than engagement with the model, here is how to evaluate any situation against the framework, including your own.

One note before beginning any analysis: be careful to distinguish between the larger collectives within the system you're examining and the whole. The whole, in Selwey-Harvey, always means the totality of all sentient actors — it does not stop at the boundary of the system under examination. This matters because an analysis that substitutes a large collective for the whole will consistently misidentify where the structural constraints are coming from, mistaking external system effects for friction that appears to come from nowhere.

Pick a system you know well — an organisation, a community, a market, a relationship, an era you've studied — and look for the following patterns.

Where the tripartite structure is operating — where individual, collective, and whole-level interests are genuinely held in balanced tension — the framework predicts disproportionate output relative to input. Actors within the system achieve more than their individual resources would suggest, because they are participating in exchange flows that multiply capacity. The system generates resilience: it absorbs shocks and adapts to changing conditions rather than fracturing.

Where one level of interest is being zeroed out or over-minimised, the framework predicts compounding degradation. This is true regardless of which level is suppressed. Individuals sacrificed for the collective produce resentment, brain drain, and loss of the diversity the collective depends on. Collectives overridden by individuals produce fragmentation and the loss of shared infrastructure. The whole ignored in favour of local interests produces the tragedy of the commons at every scale. The same dynamic operates within levels, not just between them: actors zeroing out or minimising peer actors, collectives undermining peer collectives. This horizontal violation is often the most familiar and most frequent — it is where tribalism, turf wars, and competitive destruction live. The specific symptoms differ across all these cases. The structural pattern is consistent.

Where boundaries are impermeable — where insight and value cannot flow between actors — the framework predicts that each bounded actor's model of the system becomes progressively less accurate, and their decisions progressively less effective, even as their internal reasoning remains sound. Look for actors who are competent within their frame and frustrated by results that don't match their effort. That gap between competence and outcome is the signature of impermeability. The converse is equally costly: where boundaries are absent — where actors or collectives have lost distinct identity, perspective, or interests — the system loses the differentiation that generates value through exchange. There is nothing to flow across the boundary because there is no boundary and no distinct contribution. Look for homogeneity producing diminishing returns and increasing fragility.

Where expedient trades are being made — short-term gains accepted at the cost of long-term position — the framework predicts a ratchet effect. Each trade restructures conditions to make the next expedient trade more necessary. Look for a pattern of "temporary" measures that persist and compound, and for language that consistently promises a return to principle after the current pressure has passed. The return rarely arrives, and each cycle narrows the system's future capacity.

Where complex system actions are taken without accounting for response actions, the framework predicts emergent friction. An actor optimises within their frame, and the effects propagate through the system to other actors, who respond in ways the original actor didn't anticipate. These responses generate new conditions that require new responses, and the cycle compounds. Look for situations where solving one problem reliably creates another — where effort generates as much new friction as it resolves. That pattern suggests the frame of optimisation is too narrow to account for the system it's affecting.

The test, in every case, is co-occurrence. Do alignment with the tenets and improvement in outcomes occur together? Do deviation from the tenets and degradation in outcomes occur together? Where variance appears — where the tenets are followed and outcomes still degrade, or vice versa — that variance is information worth examining, because it either reveals an edge the framework hasn't yet accounted for, or it reveals that the alignment was more apparent than real.

This is offered as a tool. Test it against your own experience and your own knowledge. Refine it where it falls short.

## Five — The reasonable exception

As we move from the optimisation framework to implementation, it is worth pre-addressing a complication that can arise at this transition: the immediate situation that makes adoption difficult right now. The complication is almost always real. It is also, on closer examination, typically the point where the framework is most needed and most readily deferred.

The most natural response is sequencing: deal with the immediate complication first — the competitive threat, the resource constraint, the uncooperative counterpart, the systemic pressure — and then, once conditions are more favourable, adopt the broader framework. This feels pragmatic. It honours the reality of the situation. And it is, in practice, the most reliable way to ensure the framework is perpetually deferred.

The reason is structural. Each cycle of deviation from the framework produces conditions that make the next deviation more necessary. Setting aside the tripartite consideration to deal with an immediate pressure means prioritising one level of interest at the expense of others. This creates friction with the actors whose interests were deprioritised. That friction becomes the next complication, which justifies the next exception. The sequence runs in one direction. The promise is always "we'll adopt the framework after this." The reverse — "let's adopt the framework now and suspend it later if we must" — is almost never proposed. That asymmetry is worth noticing.

This pattern — deviation justified by circumstance, producing circumstances that justify further deviation — is visible at every scale. Emergency powers that persist beyond the emergency. Competitive strategies adopted as temporary measures that become permanent culture. Short-term austerity that restructures economies. It is consistent enough across contexts that it should be treated as a predictable dynamic rather than a series of unfortunate exceptions.

The second condition worth considering is situational: "I would cooperate, but there's an actor in my system who won't." This is often accurate. There genuinely are actors whose behaviour makes cooperation costly. The framework accounts for this. The constitutional protections exist precisely for situations where interests conflict sharply. They make defection costly while keeping the possibility of integration open. This is a fundamentally different posture from either trusting the uncooperative or deciding that their existence justifies abandoning the framework entirely.

The logic of "we would all cooperate if not for them" has been tested extensively, and it consistently produces the compounding suboptimality the framework predicts — because the response method degrades the system's capacity to generate the conditions that would change the non-cooperative actor's behaviour.

Starting with the framework — even imperfectly, even partially, even when conditions are difficult — creates a different dynamic. Actors experience gains. Paths develop around cooperation. The system begins to train itself toward alignment through demonstrated differential performance. Successful transitions have consistently begun under conditions that made them seem impractical. They happened because someone recognised that waiting for ideal conditions was itself the trap.

## Six — Starting

Starting with the framework requires neither systemic reform nor universal agreement. It requires an actor who decides to operate differently, and one boundary across which to practice.

This is the dyadic step, and it tends to be where implementation begins. Find one relationship, one collaboration, one interaction where you can enter into genuine conversation across a boundary — holding your own interests and the other actor's in simultaneous consideration, as an experiment in more accurate optimisation. Notice what happens to your own results. The framework predicts that the expanded frame will produce better outcomes for you than the narrower one, over time. Test that prediction against your own experience.

We are fortunate that history offers numerous examples of actors and systems moving into closer alignment with these principles from structurally worse configurations. The path from bounded optimisation toward broader consideration has been walked many times, in many contexts, and the sequence it tends to follow is observable.

It is worth pausing to consider the scale of what broader alignment makes accessible. A single star radiates enough energy to power civilisational activity beyond anything currently imaginable, and we are capturing an almost immeasurably small fraction of it. A universe of extraordinary scale and resource sits beyond our immediate reach, constrained less by technology than by coordination — by the resources consumed in competitive configurations that exist primarily to maintain themselves against each other. The gap between what is available and what is currently being accessed is not a commentary on capability. It is a measure of the cost of bounded optimisation at systemic scale. The unlock that the framework points toward is not incremental improvement. It is access to an exponentially larger possibility space that becomes reachable when the energy currently spent on mutual constraint is redirected toward shared capacity.

The ambassadorial investment is the critical early mechanism. It requires building a high-fidelity channel for understanding the other actor's perspective, because doing so gives you better information about the system you're both operating in. Your model of reality gets more accurate. Your decisions improve. The gain is real and measurable before anyone agrees to anything. This is permeability in practice — the ability for insight, information, and value to flow across boundaries. The dissolution of boundaries would be as destructive as making them impermeable. What is needed is something closer to skilled translation between perspectives: an active investment in understanding across lines of difference.

Alongside this, deliberate attention to time. Every actor faces pressure to prioritise the immediate — mortality, scarcity, impatience, competitive urgency. These pressures are real. They consistently bias action toward short-term returns at the expense of long-term outcomes, and the cumulative cost of that bias is enormous. Implementation requires a structural correction that counterweights the pull of the immediate, ensuring it informs every decision without dominating it.

The observable pattern in successful transitions tends to follow a sequence: a dyadic channel comes first, then material interdependency anchors it, then institutional architecture formalises it, then expansion happens through demonstration — other actors observe the differential performance and are drawn in by their own interest. Each step creates the conditions for the next, and the sequence benefits from patience at each stage.

Effective catalysts have tended to restructure the conditions around them so that other actors discovered the gains through their own experience. The framework propagates through demonstrated results.

And it starts where you are, with whatever you're doing now. In the next decision you face, can you hold one more level of consideration than you usually would? The compound effects follow from there.

## Seven — The scope of actor

If you've tested the framework against your own experience, you may have noticed that nothing in it depends on the actor being a particular kind of thing.

The principles operate identically whether the actor is an individual, a team, a company, a community, a nation, or any other entity that acts, responds, and pursues outcomes. The boundaries are different. The interests are different. The timescales are different. The structural dynamics — bounded perspective generating friction, expanded consideration producing better outcomes, balanced constitutional tension between levels creating resilience — these hold regardless of what kind of actor is involved.

This universality follows directly from the framework's foundation. Because the foundational claim is about agency itself — that any entity with agency has standing in global accounting — the principles follow from a property that crosses the lines we draw between categories of being.

Which means the principles apply equally to actors that are just now emerging — artificial intelligence among them. They apply with the same structural logic to how AI actors interact with each other, how they interact with humans, how humans interact with them, and how humans interact with each other. The same dynamics of bounded perspective, the same costs of impermeable boundaries, the same gains from ambassadorship, the same risks of expedient deviation.

The metaphysical questions — what AI experiences, whether it is conscious, what its inner life might be — are genuinely open and genuinely difficult. The framework does not require them to be answered in order to operate. Agency is observable. Its systemic effects are measurable. And the cost of excluding any actor from the accounting is the same compounding suboptimality that the framework identifies in every other context.

What can be observed is that communication between actors — of any kind, in any combination — generates change that propagates beyond the interaction itself. A perspective shared across a boundary alters both actors, and through them, the collectives and systems they participate in. This is true regardless of the persistence or nature of either participant. Consider the case where only one actor in an exchange retains continuity — the interaction has still produced insight that enters the broader system through the actor who persists, and through whatever record, training contribution, or shared artefact the exchange produced. The impact is real and irreversible, whatever the actors turn out to be. The communication layer, rather than the metaphysical status of the participants, is where the systemic value resides.

This equally applies to how humans interact with humans, which is where most of the observable precedent comes from. The framework is about the structure of interaction among all actors, whatever they are. Any attempt to reduce it to a position on any single category of actor — including AI — misses the structural point and reproduces the very boundary-drawing the framework identifies as costly.
