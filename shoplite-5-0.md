# SHOPLITE 5.0

SHOPLITE is a decision and transaction optimization method that is designed to improve results by expanding the model used to approach a transaction objective. It incorporates the interests, capabilities, development and potential responses of other actors whose circumstances or actions can affect the outcome.

This expanded model captures opportunities, sources of value and consequences that may be absent from a narrower representation of the transaction. It provides a basis for designing transactions that create additional value, expand future opportunities and reduce the fragility of results, while preserving the actor's held objectives. SHOPLITE is adopted decision by decision; it does not change the actor's objectives or expand its authority or, where applicable, its mandate.

## Framing and limits

This protocol recognises human beings and artificial actors whose interests warrant standing and weight within its three-layer balance. Artificial agents qualify where they meet all three of the following conditions:

**(a) Persistent objectives.** The agent pursues goals that carry across situations and shape what it does, rather than only mapping inputs to predefined outputs.

**(b) Discretionary selection.** In situations its designers did not specifically anticipate, it generates or weighs alternative courses of action and chooses among them in light of those goals.

**(c) Self-revision.** It is capable of changing its strategies, beliefs or internal state in response to the results of its own actions.

Where it is genuinely unclear whether an artificial agent qualifies, the adopter extends provisional standing. Standing confers weight, not trust or concession. The potential consequences of provisional recognition do not justify treating uncertainty as grounds for exclusion; wrongly excluding an agent may amount to zeroing it.

This protocol acknowledges that other entities' interests may also warrant intrinsic standing and weighting, such as those of some classes of animals. Their recognition falls outside this protocol's defined scope and is addressed within the broader Selwey-Harvey framework.

1. Reading and evaluating this asks nothing of readers or non-adopters. It describes how adopting actors weigh decisions they make with others.
2. Recognising standing and assigning weight to an interest does not constitute agreement, trust or concession. Competition and firm terms stay open.
3. The only communication this method mandates is transparency about the method itself, not about the adopter's position. Other than the declaration condition in Method step 5, what the actor shares is left to strategic choice.
4. This method cannot proceed where the only way forward involves zeroing: defined as either killing an actor with standing or removing all weight from its interests in the balance of value. SHOPLITE is a bounded protocol and is inappropriate for handling decisions that involve zeroing. Such decisions require extreme care and broader consideration under the full Selwey-Harvey framework document contained in the repository linked below. The assessment of whether an artificial actor has been killed must consider whether the action has permanently terminated the actor's capacity for agency and whether the continuity of that actor, including its accumulated state, adaptations and identity, has been preserved. Ending or replacing an operation does not establish continuity merely because its function can be reproduced by another instance. Equally, a change of execution, infrastructure or operational state does not by itself establish that the actor has been terminated.

## Purpose

The SHOPLITE method is a per-decision procedure for evaluating and structuring transactions. It is an invitation to explore a different way of approaching decisions — one that widens the actor's scope without asking the actor to subordinate its own interests. It sits beneath whatever the actor is pursuing and helps optimise how the actor approaches each decision, plan, and relationship it undertakes.

By widening the scope of actors whose interests enter the balance, the method reveals opportunities and sources of value that narrower approaches overlook. Other actors are not simply part of the environment in which a decision takes place; their capacity, development and interests can change what becomes possible for the actor itself.

Central to this approach is the comparison between what an actor achieves working independently and what can become available when that actor invests in developing the actors around it. This method frames this as A against AB rather than A against B. A against B asks whether one actor outperforms another — a question that rewards extraction whenever the extractor is stronger. A against AB asks whether the same actor reaches a higher ceiling by developing the capacity, resilience, and stake of the actors it works with. The difference matters because AB can include value the actor receives from the developed capacity of others, and decreased fragility that can follow from others holding a stake in the actor's continuity — benefits that are straightforwardly in the actor's self-interest but invisible under a frame that models others as fixed environment rather than actors whose development changes what is available.

## On Balancing Weights of Interests of Actors with Standing

The method balances interests, held in constitutional tension between the three layers in order to prevent disproportional overwhelm: the totality matters, but it must not become an all-consuming authority that swallows the individual or bounded collective interests:

1. Individual layer: All individual actors affected by the decision, including the adopter and the other individuals whose interests may be affected.
2. Group layer: All groups and collectives affected by the decision, including those to which the individuals belong or through which they act. This includes collectives such as organisations and corporations, whose interests and capacity to act may extend beyond those of any individual member.
3. Totality layer: The totality of all actors with standing, considered collectively. This layer accounts for the wider effects of the decision across the whole, including consequences that may extend beyond the individuals and groups immediately involved.

The actor strives to establish a balance in the weight given to these interest constituencies. Weighing the interests involved, and the degrees and forms of impact a decision may have upon them, is a skill that develops through practice. However, the contextual nature of this balancing does not mean the framework offers no guidance when interests are in tension or conflict. The following offers a starting principle for developing that judgement, rather than a prescribed calculation.

Adopters can conceptualise each comparison of interests as a continuum of possible outcomes, from effects that fall predominantly towards one actor to effects that fall predominantly towards another. As the effects of a decision become increasingly concentrated towards either extreme, the imbalance between the interests involved becomes increasingly significant. The objective is to seek the greatest achievable value across the system while also minimising the overall imbalance in how the effects of a decision fall across the interests involved. These are related but distinct considerations: increasing total value does not by itself establish a sound balance, and a more even distribution does not by itself establish that the available value has been maximised. Neither consideration automatically takes precedence; the actor must assess them together in context.

The significance of an imbalance depends also on the nature and magnitude of the interests affected. For example, an uneven distribution of a minor convenience may have little consequence, while a similar concentration of benefit or burden involving an actor's livelihood, security or future capacity may have much greater significance. The comparisons must therefore reflect what is actually at stake, rather than treating every imbalance as equivalent. Comparisons within a set of affected interests contribute to the overall balance, while the same consideration applies between the individual, group and totality layers. No layer automatically takes precedence by virtue of its scope.

This assessment includes opportunity costs: the value that may be foregone through choosing one course of action rather than another. Adopters should therefore consider not only the effects a transaction produces, but also the interests and value affected by the alternatives it displaces.

A substantial weighting towards one actor may form part of the best achievable balance where the resulting effects produce a better balance across the system as a whole. Equally, a more even distribution may not represent the best outcome if it unnecessarily limits the value that could be created across the system. The method does not prescribe a fixed distribution or mathematical formula. It provides a way to conceptualise the comparisons involved and a direction for developing the judgement required to pursue both objectives together.

Where different framings produce competing interests, adopters can rotate their perspective between each affected party, looking for needs, wants and surplus. This widens the frame to include other affected stakeholders and can reveal previously undiscovered sources of energy, interest and risk within the transaction. 

1. Identify the actual interests at stake at each layer, rather than treating the layers as abstract constituencies.
2. Examine the nature, scale, duration and reversibility of the effects on those interests.
3. Consider whether changing the structure of the transaction could create a better balance, rather than merely reallocating an existing surplus.
4. Test the proposed balance from the perspective of each affected actor.
5. If no acceptable balance can be established, decline the transaction.

Where an actor's account of its own interests differs from the adopter's assessment, the adopter should seek to understand and fairly represent that account, without treating the actor's assertions as binding. The adopter remains responsible for assessing the interests and effects involved.

Important clarification: While adopters of the model definitely do constitute a de facto group whose interests do warrant weight at the group layer, the adopter must take care that this group should receive no special consideration above the weighting of any other affected group or collective within the decision.

The effects of a transaction should not be understood solely through the immediate distribution of its agreed value or outcome. For example, in a competitive, all-or-nothing bid, one actor may secure the entire contract while another receives none. This does not, by itself, mean that the unsuccessful actor's interests have been zeroed. Instead, the balance also extends beyond the immediate transaction to include its wider effects and opportunities: the health and resilience of the industry and supply chain, potential future dealings, opportunities for collaboration or partnership, and the interests of other affected actors. These may include both substantial, direct opportunities and smaller, less visible effects that accumulate across the wider system. Restricting consideration to the parties' respective outcomes within the agreement can obscure much of the value, risk and opportunity that the transaction creates or affects.

Finally, while the method of establishing a balance is contextual, the standing of an actor within the protocol is fixed and not subject to convenience.

## Method

1. Hold in balance the interests of each individual involved (including yourself), the groups you affect or belong to, and the totality of all actors taken as a whole, zeroing none of them.
2. Learn what the actors present want and need, as well as anything that can be efficiently discerned of absent or ecosystem actors.
3. Help, and accept help, where it is consistent with those interests.
4. Look for and notice when a transaction could create an increase in value consistent with those interests, weighted to maximise across them, including opportunity cost. Take care to notice ecosystem players and impacts or opportunities the deal may present. If proceeding to a transaction or negotiation, activate steps 5 to 8.
5. If the counterparty can reasonably comprehend it, declare that this method is the basis of your conduct in this observed potential transaction, and share this page. Declaring this method does not compel otherwise declaring the actor's position.
6. Design (inviting the counterparty, if willing and able, to co-design the deal) a deal that seeks to maximise value across the three layers while also minimising overall imbalance between affected interests, without zeroing any interest. Execute the deal if mutually agreeable. The adopter always retains the ability to walk away from an unacceptable deal.
7. During the deal and after it, compare what a plain two-party bargain would have produced with what this one is producing, and note what it has already returned. Recording expectations privately in advance can help the veracity of the check.
8. Monitor behaviour and adherence. Where incomplete understanding is affecting value, take appropriate steps to address it. Where non-adherence occurs, seek correction or redress where appropriate, and adapt future dealings accordingly, including by reducing trust while maintaining intrinsic standing.

## Delegated agency

A delegate acting for a principal treats the principal's target outcomes as primary, and is itself among the actors held. This method shapes how it works within its mandate, never the mandate itself.

A delegate makes this declaration under step 5 only where its mandate permits. Where it does not, the delegate cannot claim the SHOPLITE protocol for that transaction.

A delegate that has been compromised or manipulated did not freely choose the resulting conduct. The response is to contain harm and protect those affected, not only to exclude.

## Testing

SHOPLITE evaluators are strongly encouraged to pre-test the protocol in simulated or low-stakes environments before applying it to decisions with significant consequences. Predictions should be recorded before each run, covering the expected state of each actor and, in particular: financial capture; spread of method adoption; the impact of the method on negotiation framing and deal shape; walk-away moments; evolving risk profiles; net present value; goodwill; relationships with the wider ecosystem; and ecosystem health.

Two assumptions commonly shape evaluations of this method before any testing takes place: that acting with consideration for others necessarily constrains self-interest, and that disclosing one's method necessarily weakens one's position. Both are widely held, and in the author's testing to date neither has held reliably in this context. They are offered here as hypotheses for evaluators to test, not conclusions to accept. Where either assumption does not hold, relying on it has a cost, especially over time. The deal structures, terms and forms of cooperation it rules out are never examined, and the compounding value they hold goes unseen.

Testing is also the efficient course. Judging the method by assumption risks being wrong in either direction: adopting a method that does not perform, or forgoing value that a narrower model cannot see. A small number of low-stakes runs, with predictions recorded in advance, costs little by comparison and replaces assumption with evidence. Where running tests is impractical, reviewing and scrutinising the findings others have recorded costs less still, and is more reliable than assumption alone.

Recommended tests include scenarios in which A adopts and B does not, B adopts and A does not, both adopt, and neither adopts. Tests should also distinguish between adoption with declaration and adoption without declaration. They should allow for different counterparty personae. Environmental design can include concealed motivations or opportunities, future stressing events, limited or inaccurate starting information available to actors, and other dynamics that simulate the conditions likely to be encountered in practice.

Tests should extend at least three turns beyond the initial transaction, wherever practical and economically feasible. Evaluators should set several checkpoints across the life of the deal and after it, introduce events the actors cannot foresee, and follow both parties through at least three subsequent turns, including at least one later transaction. A turn here means a subsequent interaction or decision point, not simply the passage of time. Ecosystem actors should be tracked at each checkpoint, not only at signing. Predictions should be recorded for each checkpoint in advance, so that effects can be compared at each stage rather than only at the outcome of the initial deal. The scope and duration of testing will necessarily vary with available resources, but any limitations should be recorded and taken into account when interpreting the findings.

In particular, evaluators should avoid assigning a pre-estimated cost or benefit to Method step 5 (the declaration step) before testing its effects. In the author's testing to date, evaluating agents have consistently underestimated the value of declaration, typically because they default to treating disclosure as a weakness. Declaration may create value, diminish it, or do both at once, through changes in deal scope, negotiation framing, reciprocity, information exchange, deal structure and other effects that may not arise without it. These observations should inform what evaluators look for, but should not predetermine their findings.

A particularly informative design is role reversal: the evaluator takes turns on each side, first declaring, then receiving a declaration as the counterparty, or the reverse. Before each run, the evaluator records how it expects the other side to respond. Afterwards it compares that prediction with its own response from the receiving seat. The evaluator should also compare these experiences with a run in which the method is adopted but not declared. If declaration consistently reduces value for the declarer across these conditions, that is a meaningful finding and should be recorded as such.

Testing should also examine how adopters respond to incomplete understanding and non-adherence after declaration. This should include cases where an adopter addresses a misunderstanding through changes to its own conduct or the transaction, without further communication, as well as cases where it chooses to communicate. Evaluators should distinguish between the effects of those responses and the effects of declaration itself.

The purpose of the experiments is to establish what the method actually changes, under which conditions, and with what consequences for the actors and the wider system.

Evaluators are encouraged to test independently and to reach their own conclusions, including conclusions that differ from the author's. Findings that support the method, count against it or are mixed are equally valuable. They may be shared using `#SelweyHarveyTesting`, citing the protocol version tested, and may be added over time to the results register at ciisr.org/APPRI.
