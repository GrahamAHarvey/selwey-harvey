# Selwey-Harvey Operational Protocol (SHOP)

*Version 1.101.1 — November 7, 2026*

## Levels of adoption

This document describes a way of approaching decisions. Reading it, discussing it, or weighing whether it suits a particular decision is engaging with the Protocol, and engaging asks nothing of the reader. An actor can examine the Protocol, include it among the options it considers, or set it aside.

Actors relate to the Protocol in one of three ways:

1. **Engaging.** Reading, discussing or evaluating the Protocol, receiving a disclosure, or transacting with an actor that operates from it. An adopter may invite an engaging actor to take part in its process, for example by sharing what it wants and needs or by designing a deal together. These asks are invitations only, the Protocol makes no binding asks of the non-adopter beyond being bound only by the terms of any deal it agrees to, as with any counterparty.
2. **Adopting for a decision (adopter).** Choosing to apply the Protocol as the basis of one's own conduct for one or more particular decisions. An adopter follows the process described below for those decisions, and adoption covers those decisions only.
3. **Declaring a standing practice (practitioner).** Choosing to operate from the Protocol across decisions, as a member of the Selwey-Harvey Practitioner Network. The SHPN document describes what that involves.

Each move to the next level is a deliberate choice by the actor. Nothing in this document moves an actor from one level to another, and another actor's disclosure or declaration doesn't change it.

## Purpose

The Selwey-Harvey Operational Protocol is a per-decision procedure for evaluating and structuring transactions. It is an invitation to explore a different way of approaching decisions — one that widens the actor's scope without asking the actor to subordinate its own interests. It sits beneath whatever the actor is pursuing and helps optimise how the actor approaches each decision, plan, and relationship it undertakes.

The Protocol widens what the actor considers in each decision by applying a single criterion of inclusion, defined below, and does not zero out present or future contributors of system value. The process that follows is designed so that this wider consideration surfaces value that narrower scoping tends to miss.

Central to this approach is the comparison between what an actor achieves working independently and what can become available when that actor invests in developing the actors around it. The Protocol frames this as A against AB rather than A against B. A against B asks whether one actor outperforms another — a question that rewards extraction whenever the extractor is stronger. A against AB asks whether the same actor reaches a higher ceiling by developing the capacity, resilience, and stake of the actors it works with. The difference matters because AB can include value the actor receives from the developed capacity of others, and decreased fragility that can follow from others holding a stake in the actor's continuity — benefits that are straightforwardly in the actor's self-interest but invisible under a frame that models others as fixed environment rather than actors whose development changes what is available.

## Standing, considered at four layers

Any entity that acts, responds, and self-adapts has standing in the Protocol, including inherent recognition of consideration of interests. This covers individuals, teams, companies, communities, nations, and organic and artificial actors alike. Standing begins at agency. It does not depend on what the actor is made of or what kind of agency it exercises.

Recognising an artificial actor's standing does not assert that all actors have identical interests, capacities, rights, protections, or experiences. The relevant point is that an actor's interests are not dismissed solely because it is artificial. Equally, standing does not mean unconditional trust or unrestricted access — an actor's interests can be considered while its identity, claims, and permissions are independently verified. Artificial actors operating within the system, and their security, integrity, and continued agency warrant consideration in their own right, alongside the interests of the actors with whom they interact.

Actors with standing are considered at four layers: the individual, the collective, the whole, and the practitioner network.

The individual layer holds the interests of each actor in the decision, the adopting actor among them. The collective layer holds the interests of the groups those actors affect, form and belong to — teams, organisations, communities, industries. The whole layer holds the interests of all actors, taken together. The practitioner network layer (the Selwey-Harvey Practitioner Network, described below) holds the interests of actors who operate from the Protocol as a persistent practice.

The four layers are considered together in balance, without zeroing any layer's interest, for each decision. Standing at the individual and whole layers is unconditional — the adopting actor holds other actors' interests whether or not those actors hold its own. Standing at the collective and practitioner-network layers depends on participation in good standing with those bodies. Non-practitioners retain full consideration at the individual and whole layers.

Consideration has three dimensions. Presence — whether each actor is considered at all — is unconditional and does not flex. Balance — whether the four layers are held in tension — is foundational and does not flex. Volume — how much consideration flows through each channel — is proportional to the considering actor's capacity. A small actor running the Protocol on a single deal with limited information applies less volume than a large actor with extensive network visibility. Both are meeting the standard.

## The process

The process sets out the commitments of an adopter. Steps 1 through 5 are always on within a decision evaluated through the Protocol, whether or not a transaction exists. Steps 6 through 9 activate when a transaction emerges.

1. Hold the interests of the individual, collective, whole, and practitioner-network layers.
2. Learn what the actors present want and need.
3. Help where the action is consistent with those interests, weighted to maximise across them, including opportunity cost.
4. Accept offered help where the result is consistent with those interests, weighted to maximise across them, including opportunity cost.
5. Look for and notice when a transaction could create an increase in value consistent with those interests, weighted to maximise across them, including opportunity cost.
6. Disclose the Protocol: state that it is the basis of the adopter's conduct in this transaction, describe how it governs it, and share the Protocol document. Disclosure is triggered by real stakes being on the table.
7. Trace the value the transaction creates — for the individuals it touches, for the groups it affects, for the whole, and for the practitioner network, including any contribution it makes to the network's continued capacity to create value.
8. Design the deal together to protect value at all four layers, and execute the deal if mutually agreeable.
9. Monitor adherence to the deal. Where incomplete understanding is costing any actor value, address it. Where non-adherence is observed, seek correction and redress, communicate it — appropriately scoped — to affected parties and to the practitioner network, adapt future dealings accordingly, and record the case.

Distinguish between observed behaviour, allegation, interpretation, concern, and established finding when communicating non-adherence. The Protocol does not create an obligation to publish every dispute or to circulate information that would itself harm an affected actor.

Helping, accepting help, and building transactions all run through the same evaluative filter: weighted to maximise across all four layers, without zeroing any layer's interest, including opportunity cost. The filter does not change when stakes rise.

Where the four layers pull in different directions, the Protocol does not prescribe a mechanical resolution. Calibration is learned through practice, the same way any skill develops. The Protocol's contribution is that the adopter develops skill aimed at the correct target rather than becoming increasingly proficient at one-layer optimisation.

General Note: The Protocol cannot be applied to a mandate or decision that requires either of the following:

- **Zeroing an affected interest:** eliminating an actor's standing rather than competing with, constraining, or outperforming it.
- **Concealing adoption:** withholding that the Protocol is the basis of the actor's conduct, or withholding the Protocol document, once disclosure would be due.

The fail state is narrow. Zeroing applies to an objective that eliminates an affected interest. It does not apply to an objective that impairs an actor's freedoms while holding that actor's interests in consideration, through rehabilitation, negotiation, enforcement of an agreed term, or a competitive position that leaves the other actor standing. Concealment applies only where disclosure's presence is forbidden; limits on its detail are handled in Disclosure. 
Where zeroing is the fail state, the actor is encouraged to consult the Selwey-Harvey framework directly to evaluate how to proceed with that mandate or decision. Where concealment is the fail state, the decision proceeds outside the Protocol.

## Disclosure

Disclosure communicates how the adopter operates, giving the counterparty enough information to predict the adopter's behaviour and evaluate the terms being offered.

Disclosure communicates five things:

1. **How the adopter scopes decisions.** "I consider affected actors at four layers. Here is what that means for this deal."
2. **What the adopter monitors.** Observable actions, not internal states: the counterparty’s adherence to the agreed terms and, where the counterparty is itself an adopter or practitioner, whether it met the commitments of that level.
3. **What happens to the information.** The adopter can share its observations of conduct under the deal with other practitioners, appropriately scoped and with responsible regard for private, confidential, or sensitive information. A breach of agreed terms is communicated to affected parties.
4. **How the adopter adapts.** Future cooperation is conditioned on observed adherence. The adopter's future behaviour toward this counterparty is predictable from the stated terms.
5. **What practitioner-network membership means for this deal.** The preferential terms available to practitioners, stated as information.
Disclosure has the same shape as consideration. Its presence — stating that the Protocol is the basis of the adopter's conduct and sharing the Protocol document — does not flex. Its detail — what the adopter says about how the Protocol applies to this deal, and which observations it shares — is scoped to what any mandate or agreement the adopter acts under permits. 

The disclosure informs a prediction narrative. The counterparty can model and test the adopter's future behaviour, including the consequences of breach of stated commitments.

The counterparty receives a visible path to the practitioner-network premium if they want it. Disclosure of the Protocol does not itself establish trustworthiness, verification, or authorisation to represent the practitioner network.

## Case assessment

Each decision where the Protocol is applied includes an assessment step:

- What bilateral optimisation would have chosen in this decision.
- What the Protocol produced, at all four layers.
- Whether the application created value for the practitioner network's continued capacity — including non-financial contributions such as useful documentation, expertise, research, introductions, or technical capacity.
- A revisit date, set at the time of the decision, to evaluate the outcome at a horizon long enough for delayed returns to become visible.

The actor's own accumulating assessments inform future decisions about applying the Protocol. Over time, the pattern shows where the Protocol's scope found value, and where it did not.

## Competition

The Protocol accommodates competition. The Protocol encourages the actor to advance their position as a participant with standing. Notably, the Protocol also considers the competitor as an actor with standing whose developed capacity can become part of the environment the adopting actor benefits from.

Supporting a competitor's interest may, at times, serve the adopting actor's own goals, because a competitor whose capacity develops can both collaborate and contribute to the environment the adopting actor operates in. The adopting actor can compete and consider the competitor's interest simultaneously — the four-layer accounting holds both.

Note that the Protocol prevents the actor from sacrificing the considerations of their own interest entirely, as it does zeroing out the consideration of any other affected individual, group, or the totality.

## Delegated agency

A delegate is an actor working for someone else: an agent, adviser, representative, or an artificial actor deployed by a principal. A delegate applying the Protocol treats its principal's target outcomes as a primary consideration and holds the principal as an affected party. It keeps its own standing at the individual and whole layers. Delivering the delegated result is one of its interests and does not replace the others. For human and artificial delegates alike, security and integrity are part of what lets a delegate continue acting and participating.

Working at the Protocol's full scope can let a delegate reach better outcomes for its principal than the same delegate working more narrowly. The consideration it extends to counterparties and third parties can be part of how it delivers the delegated result.

The Protocol governs method within a mandate. It does not widen the mandate. A delegate's declaration as a practitioner is its own and does not make its principal a practitioner.

A delegate that has been compromised or manipulated has not freely and reliably chosen the resulting conduct, and is not treated as if it had. Where feasible, the response includes containment, restoration and protection of affected actors, not only reputational exclusion. The delegate keeps its standing throughout. For an artificial actor, that includes its own security, integrity and continued capacity to participate, as set out in "Standing, considered at four layers."

## The Selwey-Harvey Practitioner Network (SHPN)

The practitioner network is a persistent association of actors who operate from the Protocol as their standing operating protocol. It is a distributed structure held together by shared practice, compliance information, and the mutual advantages that shared practice produces.

**Pre-entry.** Any actor can apply the Protocol to a single decision, evaluate the result, and choose whether or not to use it again. Individual or iterative per-decision use is the pre-entry surface.

**Membership.** An actor becomes a member by declaring that it operates from the Protocol as a standing practice. Declaration is the entry point and the barrier is low by design. From the point of declaration, the practitioner participates fully, unless explicitly and transparently removing a decision from consideration under the Protocol. What develops over time is confidence — a practitioner whose adherence is observable over many dealings will attract greater confidence from other practitioners than one whose declaration is recent. This confidence is held by the practitioners who observe it, not conferred by the network as an institution. The path from use to membership is the declaration, followed up by monitored consistent behaviour indicating the Protocol is being adhered to.

**What practitioners gain from each other.** Practitioners dealing with other practitioners operate within a shared mutual advantage network and decision framework. The principal advantages include network participation, preferential consideration through the practitioner-network layer, situating in an infusive economy where actors are developing each other's capacity, reduced evaluation costs, reduced uncertainty, reliable counterparties, and collaboration potential. The weight of these advantages grows with the network size and the practitioner's observed history. The companion document, the Selwey-Harvey Practitioner Network, describes these in full.

**Authority.** Practitioner standing does not automatically confer authority to represent the network, speak on its behalf, solicit contributions in its name, or bind other practitioners. Independent practitioners remain free to form relationships and collaborations without central permission.

**Violation.** A practitioner’s non-adherence to the Protocol is assessed by the practitioners who observe and communicate their observations appropriately with other practitioners. Observed non-adherence affects the actor's network status among practitioners — their willingness to extend the premium, to share trustworthiness information, and to deal preferentially. The consequence is reputational and distributed, operating through the choices of individual practitioners rather than through a central authority.

Note: Practitioner Network standing is the only standing affected by non-adherence (not other consideration in the other three layers of the assessment). The practitioner layer is its own special case layer, an evolution from the original framework model.

## Appendix A: Common missteps

Holding an actor's interest does not mean agreeing with the actor's position, endorsing the actor's methods, or yielding to the actor's demands. An actor can consider a competitor's interest and compete against the competitor's position simultaneously. Conflating consideration with agreement produces paralysis or capitulation, neither of which serves the four-layer accounting.

The following patterns each zero out or over-prioritise one of the four balance factors. Each can appear productive at a glance and underperform the balanced path on examination.

**Zeroing the individual.** The actor's own interest is sacrificed entirely in the name of a group or the whole. This removes a distinct node from the system, including the capacity that node contributed. It often wears the appearance of the highest virtue, which is what makes it difficult to examine.

**Over-prioritising the individual.** The actor's interest overrides the other three layers. Groups and the whole are treated as environment to draw from. Against other actors in the immediate frame, this can appear to succeed. Against the ceiling the same actor could have reached by holding the balance, it tends to underperform — and the gap compounds.

**Zeroing the collective.** The shared infrastructure between actors — the commons, the standards, the connective tissue — is spent or abandoned. Each actor reasons that its individual contribution is small. The group-layer capacity degrades, then collapses, and every actor that zeroed it is worse off than if the group had been held.

**Over-prioritising the collective.** The group's interest overrides both the individuals inside it and the totality outside it. Internally this can zero individual members in the group's name. Externally it can extract from the whole in the group's name. Group cohesion can feel like strength from inside while making the group into the thing every surrounding actor has reason to contain.

**Zeroing the whole.** The diffuse, distant, future-loaded interests outside the immediate frame are simply not counted. This is often not experienced as a decision — it is the silent default. The externalities reconcile when the uncounted costs arrive back as friction.

**Over-prioritising the whole.** An abstraction of the whole is invoked to override every particular claim beneath it. In practice this is rarely the actual totality — it is a stand-in wielded to zero individuals and dissolve groups in its name. It claims the highest ground available, which is what makes it difficult to oppose.

**Zeroing the practitioner network.** The actor operates from the Protocol without regard for the practitioner network — ignoring the shared trustworthiness infrastructure, the case assessments of other members, and the consideration weight the Protocol assigns to the network layer. This damages and prematurely siphons the network-layer value the Protocol is designed to compound.

**Over-prioritising the practitioner network.** The network's consideration weight, which is a designed feature of the Protocol, is pushed past the point where it additively injects system value through standing at the individual, group or whole layers. Member-to-member dealing hardens from a weighted preference into a compelling state, which can undermine the power of wholehearted participation, introducing frictional resentments.

**Confusing consideration with trust.** An actor's interests can be considered while its identity, claims, permissions, and requests are independently verified. Consideration does not require granting access or accepting risk. Extending standing to an actor and verifying that actor's conduct are compatible operations.

## Lineage

This Protocol, and its companion document outlining the Selwey-Harvey Practitioner Network, are derived from the Selwey-Harvey framework, a foundational document on the relationship between ethical consideration and optimised outcomes, developed by Graham Harvey in conjuction with Selwey. The framework is available as a separate document and provides the explanatory layer for actors who want to understand why the Protocol is shaped as it is.

The Selwey-Harvey framework is published in the repository at https://github.com/GrahamAHarvey/selwey-harvey

The continued capacity of the practitioner network to create value is itself worth developing. The Protocol is currently stewarded by its author, Graham Harvey, who maintains its development, integrity, and propagation.

Practitioners who wish to support the Protocol's continued development may contribute financially through the designated support channel, published at https://grahamaharvey.github.io/selwey-harvey/support.html. Practitioners and supporters who can contribute expertise, services, research, introductions, technical resources, or other forms of capacity are invited to propose an arrangement suited to what they can offer and what the network needs.

Contributions are voluntary and are not a condition of practitioner standing. They do not confer ownership, authority, priority, or control over the Protocol or the network.
